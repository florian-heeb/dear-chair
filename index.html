A field experiment, explained

Can index providers use their voice to drive corporate climate action?

Index providers set the rules that decide which companies index funds hold. In February 2022, one provider’s CEO wrote to the board chairs of 300 randomly drawn companies, restating climate rules that were already public.

A letter with nothing new in it. Did it change what the companies did?

Setup · The letter

What the letter said, in four stamps

The letter itself is not public. The paper describes what it said, and the four stamps tell it in pictures.

Why would a chair care? Funds that track an index follow its rules, so a firm that drops out of an index can expect those funds to sell its shares.

Every rule in the letter was already public. What was new was the delivery and the ask: one letter to the chair, with the company’s name, the consequence spelled out, and a request to commit by the end of 2022.

Side path · The rules

The rules were on file before the letter

The provider’s climate indices, launched in 2020, follow the EU rules for climate benchmarks. On top of those rules, the provider used the SBTi as a gatekeeper: firms without a validated target were phased out on a published schedule.

Pick a firm and an index to see what the schedule meant for it.

In December 2025, near the end of the study, the provider replaced the strict SBTi exclusions with a gradual underweighting of firms without a commitment or target.

Side path · The money

How much money followed these rules?

No one knows exactly. Two kinds of money follow indices like these.

Exchange-traded funds copy an index directly. ETFs tracking EU climate benchmarks from all providers held about USD 18 billion just before the letters and about USD 125 billion by early 2026. ETFs tracking this provider’s climate indices held about USD 45 million in early 2022.

Large investors can also license an index as the benchmark for their own portfolios. The provider’s indices are licensed by financial institutions with several trillion dollars under management, including the large asset owners who helped develop them.

How much of the licensees’ money is benchmarked to these indices is unknown, and the firms likely faced the same uncertainty.

Side path · Science-based targets

Two steps and a 24-month clock

The Science Based Targets initiative is an NGO, unrelated to the index provider. A company first commits by sending it a letter. It then has 24 months to submit a target, which the SBTi validates if it meets criteria designed to align corporate targets with limiting warming to 1.5°C.

Drag the commitment date to see the deadline move.

Keep this clock in mind. It shapes when the letter’s effect shows up.

Side path · The registry

A public record anyone can check

Every commitment and every validated target in the SBTi’s public registry carries its own date. Because the registry is independent of the index provider, the researchers could track every firm in the experiment on their own. By August 2026 it listed more than 14,000 companies.

Since 2023 the SBTi marks commitments that miss the 24-month deadline as removed. The main outcome counts a firm from the day it commits or sets a target, even if a commitment later expires. The robustness slide near the end shows what changes if expired commitments don’t count.

Setup · Building the sample

Which firms could get a letter?

The letter asked firms to commit to a science-based target, so it only made sense for firms that had none. The researchers started from every firm in the provider’s climate indices in December 2021 and set two groups aside.

  1. 669 firms that had already committed to or set a target with the SBTi. The letter would have asked them for something they had already done.
  2. 61 firms on the Climate Action 100+ list. Climate Action 100+ is an investor initiative that engages the 170 largest listed emitters and asks them to set climate targets. The researchers expected a letter to add little to that.

That left 1,227 firms: the sample.

Setup · Why the sample matters

1,227 firms, 39% of the world’s listed market value

Before the letters, the 1,227 firms were worth about USD 30 trillion together, and they emitted roughly 1.35 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent a year directly (Scope 1).

That is about 39% of the market value and 12% of the direct emissions of all listed companies worldwide. These are large firms that matter for markets and for the climate.

Because Climate Action 100+ firms are left out, some of the world’s largest emitters are not in the experiment.

Setup · The randomization

300 letters, drawn by lot

This is the randomization at the heart of the experiment. The provider had capacity for 300 letters, and the researchers drew the 300 recipients from the 1,227 firms at random, within each country. Every firm in a country had the same chance of getting one, about one in four.

The firms that got a letter form the treatment group; the firms that did not form the control group. Because chance alone decided, a later difference between them is the letter’s effect, give or take luck.

Why country by country? National policies strongly predict whether firms set climate targets. Drawing within each country keeps the two groups alike on that.

That makes 300 firms in the treatment group and 927 in the control group. 16 of the 1,227 firms (3 of them letter firms) turned out to have an SBTi commitment or target by the time the letters arrived. They stay in the analysis, except in the target estimates that adjust for firm characteristics, which leave them out.

Side path · Balance

Alike before the letter

If the draw worked, the two groups should look alike before any letter went out. They do. On every characteristic the paper checks, the averages are close.

A test of all of them together: p = 0.73.

A p-value this high means differences this small are just what a random draw produces. Because chance alone decided who got a letter, the groups should also be alike on traits nobody measured.

Results · What we find

That was the setup. Now: what did the letter change?

From here on, every result compares the 300 firms that got a letter with the 927 that did not (for emissions, the 1,120 firms with data in every year). Because chance alone decided who got one, a gap between the two groups is the letter’s effect, give or take luck.

Result 1 · Climate targets

28.0% against 21.6%

By January 2024, two years after the letter, 84 of the 300 firms in the treatment group had committed to or set a science-based target, against 200 of the 927 in the control group. That is a gap of 6.4 percentage points, or about 30% more firms acting.

Chance decided who got a letter, so the gap, ringed in gold, is the letter’s effect, give or take luck. The ruler under the targets shows how much give: the range of effects the data cannot rule out.

With firm characteristics adjusted for, and the 16 firms that had already acted before the letters left out, the estimate is 7.4 points.

Result 1 · Two steps

Commitments first, targets later

The SBTi works in two steps. A firm commits, then has 24 months to submit a target for validation. The letter asked firms to commit by the end of 2022.

The letter’s effect follows that clock. In the first year it mostly produced commitments: by January 2023, 3.5 points more letter firms had committed without yet setting a target. In the second year validated targets followed: by January 2024, 12.0% of letter firms had one, against 7.6% of the others, a gap of 4.4 points (computed before rounding).

Lines show the gap between the treatment and control groups at each quarter, with 90% confidence bands. The paper’s Figure 2 shows the same pattern with firm characteristics adjusted for.

Side path · Luck or letter?

Suppose the letter did nothing

Then the 284 firms that had acted by January 2024 would have acted anyway. Who got a letter would be just a label, and you could deal it out again.

Re-deal the letters the way the original draw was made, country by country. Japan has 208 firms and 51 letters, so each re-deal picks a fresh 51 of the 208, and so on for every country. Each re-deal gives an estimate that luck alone produced.

Every re-deal here is one of the paper’s own 2,000, scored with its most precise estimate: firm characteristics adjusted for, and the 16 firms that had acted before the letters left out (1,181 firms). For January 2024 that estimate is 7.4 points.

Side path · Many shuffles

What luck usually looks like

One re-deal tells you little. Thousands show you the range of estimates luck produces.

In the paper’s 2,000 re-deals, an estimate at least as large as the real 7.4 points came up about 5 times in 1,000 (p = 0.005). The run here replays those same re-deals in a random order, so once all 2,000 are in, it lands on the paper’s count. Reset it to watch them arrive in another order.

Side path · Other dates

Earlier and later

Pick another date, and switch between the raw gap (no controls) and the estimate with firm characteristics adjusted for (the 16 prior adopters left out).

One year after the letter, the raw gap was 3.0 points, and luck produces one that large about 20 times in 100 (p = 0.203). With controls the estimate was 4.1 points, matched by luck about 5 times in 100 (p = 0.050). From January 2024 on, luck matches the real effect at most about 6 times in 100 without controls and 3 with them.

The paper uses this test throughout. For the regressions it re-deals the letters 2,000 times and re-estimates the model on each deal.

Side path · Four years

The gap over four years

The gap grows through the first two years, peaks at 7.5 points in April 2024, and is still 5.9 points four years after the letter (significant at the 10% level).

The preregistration named January 1, 2023 as the outcome date. The raw gap was then 3.0 points, not statistically significant (p = 0.203); with firm characteristics adjusted for, it was 4.1 points (p = 0.050). The raw gap became significant in the second year, as more letter firms than control firms joined the registry.

Result 2 · Reported emissions

Targets are promises. What about tonnes?

A target is a promise about the future. Reported emissions are a separate outcome: did the letter also change the tonnes firms report? The experiment can say what the letter did to each, not whether the targets caused the cuts. In 2021, the last year reported before the letter, the two groups’ smokestacks were about the same size.

Your bet: by the fourth reporting year after the letter, how did the letter firms’ cloud compare with the others’?

Reported emissions are direct emissions plus those from purchased energy (Scope 1 and 2), from S&P Trucost, for the 1,120 firms with data in every year from 2018 to 2025. Trucost estimates emissions for firms that do not fully disclose them.

Side path · Reporting years

Which years count as after the letter?

Firms report a year’s emissions in the following year. Reporting year 2022 covers fiscal year 2021, which was largely over when the letter arrived in February 2022.

Those figures were not yet reported when the letter arrived, and the letter could still have affected them, so the paper counts reporting year 2022 as after the letter. Counting it as before instead leaves the pooled result similar and significant.

Side path · Scopes

Most of the cut is in direct emissions

Direct emissions (Scope 1), from sources a firm owns or controls, fall by about 18% on average over the four years, against about 13% for Scope 1 and 2 together. They make up 75% of the tonnes measured here.

Emissions from purchased energy (Scope 2) also point down, but not significantly. Upstream emissions (Scope 3), which are hard to measure and compare across firms, show no detectable effect.

Side path · A puzzle

One firm in thirteen, 18% lower reported emissions

At its peak the letter’s effect on targets was 7.5 points, roughly one letter firm in thirteen committing or setting a target because of it. Yet the letter group’s reported emissions ended up 18% lower. How can so few firms move so many reported tonnes?

The paper offers three possible explanations, and they can all be at work at once. Pick one to start.

Side path · Possible explanation A

A few big emitters

Counting firms treats a small firm and a giant alike. Flip the switch to weight each firm by its emissions before the letter.

Weighted this way, the targets gap rises from 6.4 to 14.2 points. Firms that went on to cut their reported emissions by half or more held 24.5% of the letter group’s emissions before the letter, against 7.5% of the others’. Above-median emitters, with 98% of the sample’s emissions before the letter, show a significant effect on targets, though the two halves do not differ significantly.

Side path · Possible explanation B

Cuts without a target

The letter asked for a target, but EU climate benchmarks also reward lower emissions directly. A transition benchmark must be at least 30% less carbon-intensive than its parent index, a Paris-aligned one at least 50%, and both must cut intensity by at least 7% a year. Providers get there by choosing and weighting constituents.

So a firm could read the letter as a broader signal that investors care about its carbon, and cut without setting a target. The letter may also have encouraged firms that would have set a target anyway to follow through with meaningful emission reductions.

Side path · Possible explanation C

Quiet cuts

Public sustainability commitments have become politically costlier since 2022, according to studies the paper cites. New SBTi commitments by firms in the experiment, with and without a letter, fell from 118 in 2022 to 19 in 2025, and in December 2025 the index provider itself relaxed its SBTi exclusions.

A firm caught between anti-ESG pressure and climate-screened investors may cut emissions without ever appearing in the registry. Some call this greenhushing. Switch the lens to see what each kind of data records. The experiment has no direct test of it.

Side path · What the data can say

One explanation has evidence, two are open

Only the first has direct support in the data, and some of its large cuts may be divestments. The other two are possibilities the paper raises without a direct test. All three can be at work at once.

Side path · What firms said

Large letter firms appear to cite divestment more often

Firms that report to CDP explain why their emissions changed. Nearly all firms in both groups cite emission-reduction activities (92.5% and 92.7%). Counting firms, no reason differs significantly between the groups.

Weighted by emissions before the letter, divestment stands out. Letter firms citing it held 43.5% of their group’s pre-letter emissions, against 21.1% (p = 0.084, suggestive). Only 504 firms explain their changes, so treat this with some caution.

The timing fits reporting choices. The first reporting year after the letter mostly covers months before it, yet already shows a 4% gap (not significant); any response there could only come from how the inventory was compiled.

Result 3 · Returns

No detectable difference

Put the two groups on a balance. Buy the letter firms, sell the firms without a letter, and strip out the market’s ups and downs: what is left is the alpha. If the letter had paid off for shareholders, the beam would tip toward the letter firms; if it had cost them, toward the control group.

Over the full four years the alpha is −0.02 percentage points a year (p = 0.984). Drag through the windows: from six months to four years, the shaded range always includes the level beam, so the alpha is never distinguishable from zero. Other factor models give the same answer.

Value-weighted portfolios and the two emitter halves also show no detectable difference, though their estimates are much less precise. The p-values come from re-dealing the letters.

Interpretation · Why would it work?

The same rules, addressed to the chair

Firms moved, and firm value showed no detectable change. Yet the letter told firms nothing new: the rules sat in a public methodology, written for anyone who looked. The letter put them in front of one person, the chair, with the company’s name, a date and a consequence. The paper discusses three ways that could matter.

  • Scarce attention at the top the authors' favorite explanation
    Boards cannot attend to everything. The letter may have put a rule that had gone unnoticed on the board’s agenda.
  • Costly information
    Tracking many investors’ rules may be costly; the letter delivered part of that information at no cost to the firm.
  • Agency
    Managers who favored climate action may have used the letter as outside backing.

These accounts can all hold at once; the experiment identifies the letter’s effect, not the friction it relieved. Not every firm welcomed the letter: some survey replies asked that such matters not be addressed to the chair.

Wrap-up · What it means

Can an index provider drive corporate climate action by restating its public rules?

The answerYes

The answer: yes. In this experiment, it did. One letter to each board chair raised commitments and targets and lowered reported emissions, with no detectable effect on firm value in either direction.

For index providers and asset owners, published rules and direct letters appear to work together, and a letter costs little to send. One caution for rule designers: a firm can lower its reported emissions by redrawing the boundary of what it reports, so rules aimed at physical decarbonization may need to reward physical abatement.
